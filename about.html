About

The hardest figure in a paper, made explorable

Fig.3D rebuilds figures from materials-science papers as 3D models you can turn, scrub and question, with the printed figure beside them.

Why

A good figure packs a crystal structure, a mechanism and an argument into one flat panel. Someone new to the field has to unpack all three in their head before the paper makes sense.

A companion does that unpacking in the open: one idea per step, the model moving while the paper’s own figure stays in view, so you can always see which part of the figure you are inside.

How each one is made

  • Start from the source. Every companion is built on one published figure. Each step says which panel, sentence or page it comes from.
  • Build the model. Geometry comes from the structures the paper names or from open crystallographic data. Where a model is idealised, the page says so.
  • Check it. Each model has its own automated tests, run on every change: lithium is conserved in the battery, the rocksalt percolation sweep is reproduced and recounted independently, drawn sites are measured against the cell they come from.
  • Say what it leaves out. Every page lists what its model does not show. Motion is illustrative unless a page says otherwise.

What it is not

Fig.3D is an independent project. It is not peer reviewed, and it is not affiliated with or endorsed by the authors or publishers of the papers it draws on. The models explain; they do not predict.

If something here is wrong, I want to know: open an issue.

Who makes it

Fig.3D is made by Marc Boyer. I choose the papers, decide what each companion should show and how it walks a reader through the figure, and review every page before it ships.

The site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with Three.js, with no build step: what you load is what was written.
