Rocksalt Li Pathways

How does lithium get through a cathode whose metals are arranged at random?

Why this paper

Most lithium-ion cathodes are ordered oxides built on nickel and cobalt, metals whose supply is concentrated in a few countries. Disordered rocksalts (DRX) can be made from Earth-abundant metals such as manganese. Hau et al., Adv. Mater. 2025, review what makes them work.

The puzzle

Charging and discharging means lithium hopping from site to site through the solid. Ordered cathodes have built-in lanes. In DRX the metals sit at random, so a hop is easy only where few metal atoms crowd its doorway, and lithium crosses the crystal only if enough easy hops link up. Extra lithium makes that more likely.

The rocksalt frame

All three cathode families in the paper sit on one scaffold: the rocksalt lattice, the same arrangement as table salt. Oxygen ions (red) fill one grid. Metal ions fill a second grid that interlocks with it. They are silver here, because we have not yet decided whether each one is Li or a transition metal.

Each metal site is wrapped by six oxygens that form an octahedron, so it is called an octahedral site. Li and transition metals (TM) both live in these sites.

Layered, spinel and DRX cathodes differ only in which metal sits on which octahedral site. The oxygen scaffold is the same in all three.

Key ideaOne fixed frame. The only choice is who sits where.

The gaps between: tetrahedral sites

Between the octahedra are smaller holes, each surrounded by only four oxygens. These are tetrahedral sites. They are normally empty, but they are the doorway Li uses to move.

Cut the rocksalt cube into eight small cubes. The centre of each small cube is a tetrahedral site. Four corners of the small cube are O, and they form the tetrahedron (orange). The other four corners are metal sites.

Each of the tetrahedron's four triangular faces is also a face of the octahedron around one of those four metal sites. The paper says the tetrahedral site face-shares with four octahedral sites. Those four sites are what the paper counts in the next steps.

  • Turn on the four octahedra. Each bright triangle is a face shared with the orange tetrahedron.
  • Show all eight tetrahedral sites in the cube.

Key ideaEvery tetrahedral site touches exactly four octahedral metal sites.

One hop: octahedral → tetrahedral → octahedral

Li cannot jump straight from one octahedral site to the next. It leaves its octahedron through a triangular window of three O, passes through the tetrahedral site, and leaves through another window into an empty octahedral site. The paper calls this o–t–o diffusion.

This model is the small cube from the last step: four corner metal sites around one tetrahedral site. Here one corner holds Li, and the other three are empty.

At the tetrahedral site the Li is squeezed closest to all four corners. How crowded this tetrahedral site is, and by what, controls how hard the hop is: the migration barrier.

Key ideaEvery Li hop goes through a tetrahedral site, and what surrounds that site sets how hard the hop is.

Counting TM neighbours

While Li is in the tetrahedral site, it is close to all four corners. A transition metal on a corner (Mn³⁺, Ni³⁺, Co³⁺, Ti⁴⁺ …) is a strong positive charge, and it pushes on the Li⁺. So each tetrahedron is labelled by how many of its four corners hold a TM.

  • 0-TMno TM corner: easiest, active
  • 1-TMone TM corner: harder, still active
  • 2-TMtoo much repulsion
  • 3-TMworse
  • 4-TMno Li corner at all, nothing to hop

From here on, “open tetrahedron” means 0-TM (and, where the paper allows it, 1-TM). The same five colours are used for tetrahedra in every later step.

Key ideaThe number of TM around a tetrahedron decides whether Li can use it. The paper counts only 0-TM and 1-TM as active.

Layered or cubic: the same doorway in a taller slab

Figure 1’s middle row treats one hop two ways. In layered oxides the 1-TM hop has “tolerable repulsion”. For spinel and DRX, whose metals sit on a cubic frame, the figure draws “Reduced c-axis”, crosses the 1-TM hop out and keeps “Only 0-TM sites utilized”.

Key ideaThe doorway is the same; the room around it is not. Layered oxides keep a taller Li slab, and the review ties that height to a tolerable 1-TM hop.

The divacancy

One more condition. A hop needs an empty site to land in. It goes most easily when another corner of the same tetrahedron is also empty. Two neighbouring empty sites are called a divacancy.

If that other corner still holds a Li, its charge crowds the tetrahedral site too. It pushes less than a TM, but it still raises the barrier.

The paper describes Li migration in layered cathodes as divacancy hops, citing refs 5, 8, 21 and 22 (p. 2), and says DRX works the same way (p. 2). It does not itself compare one empty corner with two, so that comparison is schematic here. By our reading, it also means how easily Li moves depends on how many Li are present at that moment.

Try this

  • Compare “two empty” with “one empty”: the same TM count, but in one a Li still sits on a corner beside the doorway.

Key ideaA hop needs an open tetrahedron and room around it: an empty landing site plus, ideally, one more empty corner.

Islands and highways

One easy hop is not enough. To charge and discharge, Li has to travel from deep inside a particle to its surface, hop after hop. That needs a chain of open doorways that connects all the way across.

This grid shows the idea with no chemistry. Each dot is either open (lit) or blocked (dark). Neighbouring open dots join into an island, shown in one colour. Only an island that reaches from one edge to the opposite edge lets something travel all the way through. That is called percolation.

On this square grid the switch comes near 59% open. Below that, even lots of open dots stay stuck in islands. Above it, one island suddenly spans the grid. That tipping point is the percolation threshold.

Key ideaWhat matters is not how many open doorways exist, but whether they connect into a path across the particle.

Layered: flat highways

Back to the real crystal. In layered cathodes such as NMC and LiCoO₂, the metals sort into flat sheets: a whole plane of TM, then a whole plane of Li, and so on (space group R-3m).

From the review: in layered structures only 1-TM and 3-TM environments exist, and the 1-TM channels form a 2-D percolating network. With a typical Li slab spacing of 2.6–2.7 Å, the migration barrier is below 500 meV.

The crystal here is drawn from the measured LiCoO₂ cell that step 5 sets beside the ideal cubic frame.

Key ideaLayered = 1-TM doorways that connect within each Li sheet: 2-D transport.

Spinel: highways in 3-D

Spinel (Fd-3m) keeps the same frame but orders the metals in a 3-D pattern instead of sheets. Shown here is lithiated spinel, Li₂M₂O₄, the state Figure 1 draws, with every Li on an octahedral site: TM take one set of octahedral sites (16d) and Li the other (16c).

From the review: spinel ordering creates 0-TM, 2-TM and 4-TM environments, and the 0-TM tetrahedra give low-barrier 3-D percolation channels, which makes spinel a high-rate cathode. For Mn-based spinel-like DRX, the review places Li on these octahedral, 16c-like sites at about 3 V; at about 4 V, before that further lithiation, Li sits in the 0-TM tetrahedral 8a-like sites instead (p. 12).

Key ideaSpinel = 0-TM doorways built into the pattern, connected in all three directions.

Disordered rocksalt: will it connect?

In DRX (Fm-3m) there is no pattern. Each metal site holds Li or TM by chance, so every tetrahedron type turns up, 0-TM to 4-TM. Only the 0-TM ones reliably carry Li: for spinel and DRX, Figure 1’s middle row draws a “Reduced c-axis” arrow, crosses out the 1-TM hop that layered oxides tolerate, and notes “Only 0-TM sites utilized”. The text is less strict: it counts 1-TM hops as active too (p. 2), and its 9% figure is for 0-TM networks (p. 3). The hop-rule switch below tries both. The 0-TM tetrahedra are scattered at random, so the question from step 6 comes back: do they link across the particle?

From the review: Monte Carlo percolation simulations show that a fully random cation arrangement needs at least about 9% Li excess for a percolating 0-TM network. DRX materials typically use 10–20% Li excess, and early DRX made without Li excess gave low capacity.

In the small 3–6 cell crystals drawn here, only about one random crystal in five has a network that crosses it along x at 9% Li excess, and the switch is spread from roughly 0% to 30%. That is a finite-size effect of this model, separate from the review’s point that nano-sized particles need less Li excess (p. 3). The crystal you see first was picked because its network first crosses it along x at 8.5–9%; most random crystals do not. Press “New random arrangement” a few times to see the spread, and use the chart in the next step to compare many crystals at once.

Key ideaMore Li → more 0-TM tetrahedra → the islands join into a network that crosses the particle.

Real DRX: particle size, and what this model leaves out

The 9% figure is for perfectly random cations. Real materials are rarely perfectly random, and small particles need less Li excess.

Particle size. Li only has to reach the nearest surface. In a small particle a patchy network can be enough, so nano-sized DRX can work with less Li excess.

Short-range order (SRO). Real DRX has no long-range pattern, but neighbours still have preferences. The review reports that most kinds of SRO found in DRX tend to reduce the percolation of 0-TM channels (p. 3), and that SRO can shift the Li excess needed to percolate (p. 9). This page does not model it: every crystal here is fully random.

Key ideaReal Li transport depends on Li excess + short-range order + particle size + site-energy disorder, not Li excess alone.

One figure
Four ideas at once

The review sums up how lithium moves through three cathode families in a single image. It is dense: each row is a different idea, and it assumes you already know them. This page takes the figure apart and rebuilds it in 3D, one idea per step.

“Li in layered structures migrate between octahedral sites via an intermediate tetrahedral site (o-t-o diffusion) through a di-vacancy mechanism. The migration barrier of this hop is determined by the number of TM that face-share with the intermediate tetrahedral sites.”

Section 1, Introduction · p. 2
Read the full passage

“The comparison of crystal structures and Li transport mechanism is summarized in Figure 1. In layered cathodes (R-3m), oxygen occupies the FCC lattice, and the cations (Li and TM) occupy the octahedral sites, with the Li and TM octahedra order in alternating (111) planes. Li in layered structures migrate between octahedral sites via an intermediate tetrahedral site (o-t-o diffusion) through a di-vacancy mechanism,[5,8,21,22] The migration barrier of this hop is determined by the number of TM that face-share with the intermediate tetrahedral sites. In the ordered layered structure, only 1-TM (1 TM and 3 Li face-share with the tetrahedral site) and 3-TM (3 TM and 1 Li face-share with the tetrahedral site) environments are present, but the 1-TM channels form a 2-D percolation network. With a typical slab distance of 2.6–2.7 Å, the migration barrier is below 500 meV for layered structures.[8] Spinel structures (Fd-3m) present an ideal cation ordering in FCC framework, as the TM occupy on the 16d octahedral sites creates 0-TM, 2-TM, and 4-TM environments.[23] The intrinsic 0-TM tetrahedra provides low barrier 3-D percolation channels in spinel structures, making spinel a high-rate cathode materials. With the same FCC framework, DRX (Fm-3m) has no long-range cation ordering, creating all possible tetrahedral sites with 0-TM, 1-TM, 2-TM, 3-TM, and 4-TM as face-sharing neighbors.[5–8]

Similar to layered structures, where Li migrates through a di-vacancy mechanism, the migration energy depends on the number of TMs present in the octahedra that face-share with the tetrahedral site. Among these, only 0-TM and 1-TM are considered active, as the other tetrahedral sites have too strong an electrostatic repulsion between Li and face-sharing TM for Li ions to migrate through them. Therefore, a percolating network of octahedral sites linked by 0-TM and 1-TM channels is required for Li to diffuse through DRX.[24]”

Section 1, Introduction, pp. 2–3. Reference numbers are the paper’s own.
