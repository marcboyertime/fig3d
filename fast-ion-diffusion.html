Paper companion · He, Zhu & Mo · Nature Communications 2017

Lithium that moves together

How can five ions moving at once face a lower energy barrier than one ion moving alone?

A few solid electrolytes let lithium through almost as freely as a liquid does. Using quantum-mechanical simulations, the authors found that in these super-ionic conductors the ions rarely hop one at a time. Neighbouring ions hop together, and the energy barrier for that collective step is far lower than the crystal’s single-ion landscape would suggest.

The paper

The authors simulated three fast conductors, the sulfide LGPS, the garnet LLZO and the phosphate LATP, atom by atom at 300–1,500 K. The ions hopped in correlated groups, two to three at a time on average at 900 K. For one typical group in each material they then computed the lowest-energy route and its barrier.

What you’re looking at

One stretch of the garnet Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂. Lithium (green) sits in tetrahedral T sites, with four oxygen neighbours (yellow), or octahedral O sites, with six. The channel and viewpoint are rebuilt from the crystal structure and fitted to the paper’s own drawing. Ion motion between the start and end is interpolated, and the event coordinate is not time.

The central idea

The landscape
is not
the whole story

A crystal’s framework sets an energy landscape: the energy of one lithium ion at each point along a channel. The classical picture says the highest point of that landscape is the barrier every ion must climb, 0.58 eV in LLZO.

But in these conductors there is more lithium than the low-energy sites can hold, so some ions sit on high-energy sites, pushed there by their neighbours. When a group moves together, those ions roll downhill while others climb, and their repulsion keeps the group spaced. The group’s barrier, 0.26 eV here, is lower than the landscape’s. The paper’s computed barriers agree with activation energies from its molecular dynamics and from experiments, but one event like this does not by itself set a material’s conductivity.

What the model is

The garnet channel is rebuilt from the published space group of cubic LLZO. Its orientation is fitted to the oxygen positions drawn in Figure 3b’s inset, which it matches to 0.07 Å. The five ions’ start and end sites follow the paper’s description and that inset. Their straight-line motion is an interpolation, not the calculated path.

The 1D model is the paper’s own (Methods, eqs 6–8), re-run here. It reproduces Figure 4c and 4d to within 0.07 eV.
